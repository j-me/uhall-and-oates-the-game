# Uhall & Oates III: The Sound of Moving On

## Premise

It is 2008. The label found inside Joe Timmins’s revised handbook leads John Oates to one last piece of misfiled cargo: an unopened rehearsal reel from before Uhall & Oates became a moving company.

The reel is not a lost hit. It is evidence of a promise John and Daryl made when they were young: make music together, and do not let anyone turn it into a product before it becomes a song.

Joe has other plans. He has booked a televised Uhall & Oates “Anniversary Relocation Spectacular,” complete with sponsored cardboard, branded back braces, and a one-time **Purpose Clause** he claims lets the company perform music only as advertising for moving services. Jesse and Joe Reardon, reduced but still impeccably dressed, want the show to prove that artists are easiest to control when they mistake an obligation for a comeback.

The final campaign asks a simpler question than the first two: after rescuing each other from crates, contracts, and time itself, what do John and Daryl actually want to do? The answer is not to save the moving company. It is to make a new song together.

**Format:** PG-13 comic point-and-click adventure; six chapters plus an epilogue.

**Writing rule:** All named performers are fictional comic depictions in fan-fiction. Use original dialogue and song-title allusions only; never use song lyrics.

## Theme and character payoff

- **John Oates** begins convinced that being useful means carrying every box, plan, and consequence. He becomes the project’s producer and arranger: the person who makes a performance possible without disappearing inside the labor.
- **Daryl Hall** begins eager for the glamour of a return but still inclined to let John solve the practical parts. He learns that songwriting means showing up before the applause, including for the cables, rehearsals, and difficult decisions.
- **Joe Timmins** treats the comeback as a chance to sell “emotional relocation packages.” His meddling creates the central legal obstacle, but his compulsive filing eventually reveals that he buried the proof needed to undo it.
- **Jesse and Joe Reardon** are no longer all-powerful. Their last play is to make the pair perform a sterile, pre-approved corporate jingle so that a real reunion never happens.
- **The resolution** is not a restoration of the old business model. John and Daryl choose a new, original performance under their own terms, while the moving company becomes a mundane, employee-run operation that stops pretending to be their destiny.

## What the Purpose Clause does

Joe’s 2008 corporate filing says Uhall & Oates may use the founders’ music only to promote moving services. The Reardons use that filing to program the anniversary broadcast: every original musical decision is replaced by a mandatory slogan, a sponsor cue, or an invoice notice.

The clause is legally flimsy but operationally dangerous. The broadcast system automatically cuts away from anything it cannot classify as a company jingle. To break it, John and Daryl must prove that the company was created to support their lives—not define them—and perform a new piece that contains no Reardon-owned material, no corporate phrasing, and no borrowed old contract language.

## Campaign flow

### 1. Back Together Again, on Paper

**Setting:** The Uhall & Oates depot, now embarrassingly decorated for its twentieth-something anniversary; Joe’s office; the gold Maxima’s trunk.

**Story:** The 2008 shipping label leads to the old rehearsal reel. Joe intercepts it and presents the Purpose Clause, which turns every attempt at rehearsal into a mandatory safety presentation. Daryl initially sees the coming anniversary broadcast as an easy, glamorous return. John notices the reel’s handwritten case credits both of them equally.

**Main puzzle:** Match the 2008 label to Joe’s steel filing cabinet, baffle the concrete recording corner with a packing blanket, mount the damaged rehearsal reel, then install a visibly bent take-up spool. The reel-deck controls restore studio speed, both recorded channels, and a clean monitor output. The restored reel reveals the **One on One Listening Room** in New York.

**Exit:** John keeps the rehearsal reel; Daryl agrees to attend a rehearsal as a participant, not a guest star.

**Optional comedy:** Joe’s anniversary banner can be repeatedly rehung in increasingly disastrous locations, each one triggering a different safety violation.

### 2. Private Ears at the One on One Listening Room

**Setting:** A Manhattan record shop after hours, a tiny listening booth, and Baltos’s new “audio-forensics consultancy” operating from the stockroom.

**Story:** Baltos has reinvented himself as a sound archivist because it gets him free listening copies. He recognizes the rehearsal reel but says its left and right channels were separated when the Reardons cataloged every “useful” sound as corporate property.

**Main puzzle:** Route the booth’s patch cables so John hears the practical arrangement notes and Daryl hears the melodic sketch at the same time. Each channel alone sounds incomplete; together they reveal that the original reel was meant for a live band, not a solo act.

**Exit:** Baltos reconstructs a handwritten set-list fragment and identifies a Chicago engineer who preserved the missing channel on an obsolete studio format.

**Optional comedy:** Offer Baltos different snacks from the record-shop counter; every wrong choice earns an absurdly confident, useless genre classification.

### 3. Method of Modern Music

**Setting:** A Chicago rehearsal warehouse, Michael McDonald’s analog-digital studio, and a loading dock full of unused moving equipment.

**Story:** Michael McDonald has built a studio that can transfer obsolete recordings without making them sound like a tax seminar. Michael Bolton is consulting remotely and insists the system needs “one more executive harmony.” The surviving channel is trapped behind a Reardon metadata lock that labels John as “support equipment.”

**Main puzzle:** Clip the recovered set list beside the wall-sized patch bay, take the loose three-headed cable, then build a signal chain that proves each contributor’s role: John’s arrangement grid, Daryl’s guide melody, and Michael’s transfer harmony. The rule is communicated twice—on the set list and on the patch controls—not by outside music knowledge.

**Exit:** The full rehearsal becomes playable. Its final note contains an address for a Los Angeles broadcast contractor hired by Joe.

**Optional comedy:** Turn the warehouse’s abandoned dollies into an accidentally excellent percussion section. They never become a required instrument.

### 4. I Can’t Go for That (Jingle)

**Setting:** A Los Angeles commercial studio, the Reardons’ “brand harmony” control room, and Huey Lewis Moving’s temporary sponsor lounge.

**Story:** Joe has sold the anniversary show to the Reardons as a national moving-company commercial. Huey learns that his fleet’s logo will be used as the fall guy when the broadcast goes wrong, again. He agrees to help only if nobody calls it a partnership.

**Main puzzle:** Find and load the blank four-box cue card, then replace four obviously corporate audio tags with harmless production cues: applause, studio tone, a truck reverse beep, and an invoice stamp. The goal is not to make a jingle; it is to make the system believe the broadcast has already complied while John and Daryl reserve one unscheduled live slot.

**Exit:** Huey steals the broadcast route card. It points to the final relay at Old Orchard Beach Pier, where the company began.

**Optional comedy:** Use Huey’s branded moving pads on props around the sponsor lounge to make each sponsor look increasingly ridiculous.

### 5. Out of Touch, In Rehearsal

**Setting:** A seaside rehearsal tent at Old Orchard Beach, Palace Playland-style arcade backstage, and the pier’s temporary broadcast truck.

**Story:** Returning to the pier makes the first abduction feel very close. Daryl finally admits that singing was easier than lifting because, if the performance failed, he could pretend it was never work. John admits that doing everything himself let him avoid asking whether anyone wanted his ideas. They decide that their new song must sound like both of them.

**Main puzzle:** Plug the Old Orchard route card into a visible portable broadcast board, then load the unsponsored rehearsal mix onto the four glowing floor lanes. A forgiving rhythm game asks the player to hit 9 of 12 falling notes using the on-screen lanes, arrow keys, or A/S/K/L. Misses are harmless, controls remain visible, and a failed take can be restarted immediately.

**Exit:** They create an original, unnamed song arrangement. Joe discovers it has no approved slogan and activates the Purpose Clause’s broadcast lockout.

**Optional comedy:** The arcade crane can dispense exactly the wrong stage accessory for every famous “rock” pose.

### 6. Do What You Want, Be What You Are

**Setting:** Old Orchard Beach Pier at night; the broadcast stage; the Reardon control truck; the original crane arcade; and the audience-facing sound tower.

**Story:** The final broadcast begins as Joe’s sponsored anniversary special. Work from the earlier chapters has already secured the clean live mix and its route to the pier. The Reardons expect the remaining Purpose Clause to turn John and Daryl into background to their own branded event. Joe accidentally drops the missing pre-company performance receipt while trying to expense the stage.

**Final puzzle:** A staged live-performance sequence with three distinct tasks:

1. **John’s board:** route the live instruments away from the corporate jingle bus.
2. **Daryl’s mic:** choose the original, non-contractual performance channel rather than any archived recording.
3. **The purpose file:** first insert Joe’s own receipt into the visible ORIGINAL PURPOSE slot, proving the company was incorporated after John and Daryl were already performing together.

The player can see all three stations at once, receives status feedback from the performers, and can retry any station without restarting the concert. The system fails because it cannot classify a shared, new performance as a moving-service advertisement or a Reardon-owned archive.

**Resolution:** The pair play a brief original, non-lyrical musical sting. The audience responds to the people onstage—not the company brand. The Reardons’ last control screen changes from “ASSET CLASSIFIED” to “ARTIST UNAVAILABLE.” The performance gives them the confidence to finish their first new full song together.

## Epilogue: The Things That Move Us

At the quiet depot the next morning, John and Daryl put a hand-painted sign over the Uhall & Oates logo: **CLOSED FOR REHEARSAL.** The moving company keys go to a relieved staff collective, with Huey insisting he is only providing “competitive operational advice.”

Joe Timmins unveils a new business plan—“Joe Timmins: Executive Producer of the Road Case”—and is immediately handed a real box to carry. For once, he has no one else nearby to delegate to.

John and Daryl load a small van with instruments, not furniture. Daryl reaches for the lightest case; John hands him the heaviest one. Daryl takes it, grunts, and says, “Fine. But I’m still calling the encore.”

When the player takes the **CLOSED FOR REHEARSAL** sign, the depot becomes an improvised sunrise stage. John plugs in a guitar, Daryl takes the keyboard and microphone, and they debut a new original song: **“You’re Doing It, No You’re Doing It!”** They point at one another through the hook, each insisting the other has finally taken responsibility. Animated lights, musical notes, and an equalizer turn the moment into the campaign’s curtain call before the final completion panel appears.

The final image contains no sequel crate, secret label, or new Reardon scheme: just instruments, an open road, and two partners making music together.

## Puzzle standards

- Each chapter has one main 3–5 step chain, one optional comedy chain, and at least two visible clue exposures for every required solution.
- No puzzle requires knowledge of Hall & Oates songs, music theory, or external history.
- Every music puzzle uses clear labels, visible connections, or audible/visual feedback; it never asks the player to identify a melody.
- Inventory items are never silently destroyed. Failed arrangements and routes reset cleanly.
- The final performance uses player decisions from the campaign’s themes—shared authorship, live performance, and proof of purpose—rather than a hidden code.
